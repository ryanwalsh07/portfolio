01Background

Help was on the way. People just couldn’t tell.

The first version of TapSOS already let people send a 999 alert without speaking. But once it was sent, people were left with questions. Where did the alert go? Is anyone dealing with it? Is there an idea of when help will arrive?

Without answers, people couldn’t be confident that help was actually on the way, which is the one thing they need to know in an emergency. TapSOS 2.0 set out to guide people through every step and reassure them that their alert had been received and was being handled.

The brief

A fast, safe, accessible alternative to a voice call that reduces stress while giving responders accurate information.

02The problem

Emergency services still assume you can speak.

Calling 999 usually means talking to a call handler: saying which service you need, where you are and what has happened. For people who are Deaf, have hearing loss or can’t speak, that first step is the barrier.

It is also a barrier during a medical event, or anywhere that speaking out loud isn’t safe.

What success meant

Every person gets the service they need, without overcomplicating the steps to get there.

What it needed to do

  • Let people contact help without speech
  • Cut cognitive load for people under pressure
  • Meet a range of communication needs
  • Scale through a growth-ready design system

03Who it leaves out

This isn’t an edge case.

04Discovery

Evidence-based decisions, not assumptions.

Starting with where things actually went wrong for people, day to day.

Research began by finding where the real problems were: what people ran into in everyday life, and the obstacles they faced when they needed help. One came up again and again: there was little guidance on how to ask for help without using your voice.

User research and journey mapping
Understanding real moments of crisis, and where a voice call fails people.
Competitor analysis
Direct and indirect, mapping what exists and where the gaps are.
Affinity mapping
Clustering findings into the themes that shaped priorities.
Site maps and storyboards
Sketching structure and scenarios before opening Figma.
Collaborative teamwork
Bringing the wider team into the problem to align early.
Defined success criteria
Clear measures, so design choices could be judged rather than debated.

05Accessibility first

Accessibility was not an afterthought.

Accessibility was treated as a core design priority rather than a final check. Every decision was made to reduce barriers and help people act quickly and confidently when it mattered most.

Colour contrast
Legible for people with visual impairments, checked against WCAG with Stark.
Type
Sized and spaced to scan quickly under stress.
Touch targets
Generous, for people with limited dexterity.
Simple choices
Each screen stripped back to as few decisions as possible.
Status indicators
Reassurance that an action actually completed, the gap TapSOS 1.0 left open.

06Sketch to system

From lo-fi wireframes to a scalable design system.

I moved from lo-fi wireframes through mid-fidelity flows to interactive prototypes, simplifying the interactions and the hierarchy with each pass.

Colours, type, spacing, components and interaction patterns were defined early, so the product stayed consistent, accessible and ready to grow.

System foundations

  • Type scale
  • Spacing
  • Colour tokens
  • Navigation
  • Buttons
  • Input fields
  • Status states
  • Avatars

07The answer

Replace the call with pictures and short questions.

Users choose the service they need, confirm their location and describe the emergency with pictures and short questions. The alert reaches a BT 999 handler through the emergencySMS service.

  • The problem

    You can’t tell a call handler where you are or what’s happening.

    The answer

    Alerting flow

    Location, service, then what is happening, told with pictograms and yes/no questions.

  • The problem

    You can’t explain your conditions, medication or allergies.

    The answer

    Medical profile

    Blood group, conditions, medication and allergies are sent with each alert.

  • The problem

    An emergency is the worst time to use an app for the first time.

    The answer

    Practice mode

    Rehearse a full alert without contacting 999.

08Barrier by barrier

Everything a call handler would ask, answered without speaking.

Each step of a 999 call becomes a screen, in the order a handler would need it.

Barrier 1

Saying where you are

The problem
A handler needs your location first, and you can’t tell them.
The answer
The phone’s location is shown on a map with the street address. One tap confirms it, or Edit lets the user move the pin.

Barrier 2

Asking for the right service

The problem
On a call, you would say which service you need.
The answer
All four UK 999 services in a two-by-two grid of large, pictured buttons.

Barrier 3

Describing what is happening

The problem
Explaining an emergency normally takes words.
The answer
A list of pictured emergencies for the chosen service. Ambulance has 21, and there are 59 pictograms across the app.

Barrier 4

Choosing the wrong thing by mistake

The problem
On a call, a handler can check they’ve understood you. A screen can’t, unless it asks.
The answer
The chosen pictogram is shown back at a larger size, so a mis-tap is easy to catch.

Barrier 5

Answering the handler’s questions

The problem
A handler asks follow-up questions to judge how serious it is.
The answer
The questions a 999 handler would ask, answered with Yes, No or Not sure. Breathing, bleeding, then awake. Blood group, conditions, medication and allergies are sent with each alert.

Barrier 6

Knowing it’s right before it goes

The problem
Without a conversation, there’s no moment to go back over what you’ve said.
The answer
One summary card of location, service and emergency, with a clear last chance to edit before sending.

Barrier 7

Knowing help is coming

The problem
A call ends with a voice telling you what happens next. A silent alert needs its own reassurance.
The answer
A calm confirmation, links to what was sent, and a one-tap way to tell emergency contacts.

Barrier 8

Using it for the first time in a real emergency

The problem
The worst time to learn a new app is during an emergency.
The answer
The same flow with a purple Exit Practice Mode band on every screen, so people can rehearse without calling 999.

Barrier 9

Not everyone reads English

The problem
An emergency app has to work in the language people actually use.
The answer
The app is set in Noto Sans, chosen for its wide language coverage, which the app needs for its 17 languages.

09A visual vocabulary

59 pictures that do the talking.

59 pictograms let someone show what is happening without words, from a heart attack to a boat in trouble.

10Testing

Testing with the people it’s for.

High-fidelity designs were validated in usability testing with members of the Deaf community and people with a range of communication needs, working alongside interpreters. Their feedback drove multiple rounds of iteration.

An emergency app for Deaf and disabled people only works if it’s shaped by them, so each round of testing changed the design.

12Reflection

Designing beyond the interface, and letting evidence lead.

TapSOS strengthened my understanding of accessibility, collaborative product development and evidence-based design. It showed the value of validating decisions through continuous feedback from the people who rely on the product, rather than relying on assumptions.

The biggest lesson came from TapSOS 1.0 itself: sending the alert is only half the job. In an emergency, people need to know their message arrived and that help is on the way.
